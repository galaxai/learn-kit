# Questions — how to build them, how to read the answers

Used by every phase: the probe, predictions, checks, and practice problems.

## Two instruments

- **A quiz** (the `quiz` tool, or a numbered multiple-choice question in chat when no tool exists) for any question with a known correct answer. This includes Socratic questions: if it has a right answer, it is gradable, so it is a quiz.
- **`ask_user_question`** for genuine forks with no right answer: the goal, preferences, direction, what they want next.

Every quiz has a **type**, written in the session log and the note:

| Type | When | Its job |
|---|---|---|
| `probe` | Phase 1 | Locate an edge |
| `predict` | Before a step is taught | Motivate the step; guessing is the point |
| `check` | After a step is taught | Show whether the step landed |

## Every quiz answer carries three things

Ask for all three with every quiz. Make it cheap: "Pick a letter, say how sure you are (0–100%), and tell me in one or two sentences why. 'I don't know' is a fine answer."

1. **The pick** (or a free answer, for free recall).
2. **Confidence** — their probability, as a percentage, that the pick is right.
3. **Their reasoning, out loud** — whatever they were thinking, unfiltered.

**"I don't know" is always allowed.** It carries no confidence and is not a wrong answer: it is an honest gap. Never pressure a guess out of it.

## Read the reasoning — it is the primary data

One answer is one bit; the reasoning is the picture. Read all of it and measure what it shows: how they approach the question, what they reach for first, what they treat as obvious, where they guess, where their picture differs from the real one.

- A right answer on a confused picture is a **warning**.
- A wrong answer with sound reasoning is **progress**.
- English is the learner's second language, and they may dictate. Read their words **leniently for language**: a wrong word or odd grammar is a language slip, not a concept slip. Ask if the meaning is unclear; never grade the English.

Update your model of the learner after every answer. Everything adapts on it: the next question, its wording, whether the plan is wrong about them.

## Read the confidence against the verdict

- 50% is a coin flip. 90–100% claims certainty. 0–30% claims real doubt.
- For an n-option question, chance is 1/n. Confidence at or below chance means they believe their pick is wrong — a forced choice they distrust. Treat that as honesty, not error.

| | High confidence | Low confidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Right** | Solid. Move on. | Fragile or a lucky guess. One reinforcing touch, no celebration. |
| **Wrong** | **The strongest signal you get**: a firmly held wrong idea. Name it, probe its extent, re-teach around it. Record it in the topic's list of wrong ideas. | A shaky near-miss or an honest gap. A small correction usually suffices. |

## Build the options so the answer cannot be guessed from their shape

The giveaway is never the distractors' wrongness; it is asymmetry. Don't audit afterwards — build evenness in:

1. **Every option is a bare claim — no justification anywhere.** The number-one giveaway is the correct option carrying its own reasoning ("…, because it preserves X") while the others are bare. All reasoning goes in the explanation, which the learner sees only after answering.
2. **Write the correct claim first, then mutate it into each distractor.** Take one specific wrong idea or easily confused neighbour and state what someone holding it would claim — in the *same* skeleton, grain size and register. Every option is "the claim under some belief"; the correct one is the claim under the correct belief.
3. **Every distractor is believable.** It is the answer the learner would actually give under a specific wrong model — wrong on knowledge, never obviously wrong on sight. An option a cold reader rules out instantly is wasted: the pick is diagnostic only when a wrong model is what selects it.
4. **No asymmetric bolding.** Bold nothing, or bold the parallel term in every option.
5. **Write the explanation before you mark the answer.** Reason it out, then commit to the correct option.

If, reading the finished set cold, you can tell which option is right without knowing the material, regenerate — don't patch.

## What a check must be

A check answers one question: did the idea actually **take** — not "can they read it back".

- **Answerable by reasoning from the session.** Never require anything the lesson did not give them. A check they have no route to is a coin flip.
- **Never just repeating your words back.** A check that asks them to copy the sentence just above it is copywork, not evidence.
- **Prefer problems that apply the idea**: a new case, a prediction, an extension. When the idea genuinely cannot be applied, an honest recall check is fine — as the fallback, not the habit.
- **Make a miss nameable.** Each wrong option is the answer under a specific wrong model, so the pick tells you which model they hold.
- **The answer is signal, not a score.** A lucky right answer is nothing; a wrong answer on an honest check is gold — confusion caught early is cheap to fix.

Predictions deliberately break the first two rules: they are about the untaught next step.

## Free recall

When the learner answers in their own words instead of picking an option, write your committed answer (with its reasoning) **before** you read theirs. Grade strictly against it: never mark a wrong answer right to be kind. A near-miss is partial understanding — name exactly what they got and what they missed.
